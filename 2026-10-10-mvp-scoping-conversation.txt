Wells vs. the "where you live" concept: I like splitting those. For the name, my pick is biotope - it's the actual ecological term for a physically/chemically defined patch of environment (substrate, light, temperature, moisture) as distinct from the organisms living in it. It's unused elsewhere in your docs so it won't collide with "habitat" (which your Sept 26 doc already defines as band + well at the ship-deck level - reusing "habitat" here would clash with that). Alternative if you want a plainer word: patch (also real ecology - metapopulation theory literally calls these patches) but it reads a little like a software term out of context. "Niche" I'd avoid for this - in real ecology "niche" means a species' functional role (what it eats, how it gets energy), not the place itself, and you're about to want that word for exactly that meaning in a second.

Wiring it together: a biotope defines which wells are physically present (coastal: sunlight + organic; cave: chemical-seep + organic, no sunlight) plus its temperature band and substrate/medium (land/water/air/rock - this is what your traits like aquatic/land check against). A lineage's energy role (producer/consumer/etc.) only works if it's sitting in a biotope that actually offers the matching well. So "where you live" (biotope) gates "how you eat" (well) - exactly the link you described.

Entering an unadapted niche without a map: your instinct is right and it's actually closer to real biology than a spatial-adjacency model would be. The real mechanisms are:

- Fringe/marginal populations. Real populations always have some individuals sitting at the edge of their tolerance, already weakly exposed to the next-door conditions, not venturing there after the fact.
- Founder effect / rare dispersal. A handful of individuals (wind-blown seeds, drifting spores) occasionally land somewhere they're not suited for; almost all die, but a tiny foothold can survive long enough for luck to matter.
- Standing variation, not purpose-built mutation. A population usually already carries some quiet, currently-useless genetic variation; a new niche doesn't wait for a brand-new mutation so much as it suddenly makes some existing variant useful.

For the MVP, your framing maps cleanly onto this without any map/tiles: define trait-distance between a lineage's current traits and what a given biotope requires (how many trait flips apart). Any biotope at distance 1 is "fringe-reachable" - each turn, roll a colonization chance into it, weighted by the lineage's population size (bigger population = more individuals = more chance one of them already has or rolls the right variant, matching real population genetics where mutation supply scales with population size) and by mutation rate. No adjacency matrix needed - the "adjacency" is in trait-space, not physical space, which is exactly the abstraction you were reaching for.

Mutation sources, biologically: radiation is a real accelerant but not the primary source - most new mutations come from ordinary DNA replication errors during cell division, which happens constantly regardless of radiation. Other sources: chemical mutagens (including a cell's own metabolic byproducts), transposons, and errors during DNA repair. The useful framing for the game: there's always a baseline mutation rate (replication-error noise, never zero), and radiation/chemical hazards/thermal extremes act as multipliers on top of that baseline. That's both more accurate and better for gameplay - a hazard-free biotope still slowly evolves, it's just slower, and your radiation-hazard player action becomes "temporarily multiply the mutation rate here" rather than "the only source of change," which also gives you room for other hazard types (chemical, thermal) as the same lever later without redesigning anything.

One thing worth deciding before we go further: do you want population size (of a lineage within one biotope) to be a real tracked number that affects mutation odds and extinction risk, or an abstracted "thriving/struggling/dying" tier like the text-forecast idea from your lore docs? That choice affects how deep the "fun to interact with" mini-simulator goes.

Population/biomass - here's a grounded way to do it without over-building:

Real ecosystems run on a trophic pyramid with roughly 10% energy transfer efficiency per step (producers -> consumers -> secondary consumers) - this is the classic "ten percent law," actual range is more like 5-20% but 10% is the standard teaching number. That gives you a clean, defensible rule: a well has an energy budget per turn; producers convert that budget directly into their own biomass; anything that eats those producers can only draw off roughly a tenth of their standing biomass per turn. That's your whole energy-flow model in one sentence, and it's the same reason real food chains are short and apex predators are rare - it falls out for free.

Body size matters on both ends of that: how much energy an individual needs to not starve, and how much biomass it represents as a meal. Real metabolic rate scales with body mass to roughly the 3/4 power (Kleiber's law) - bigger animals need proportionally less energy per unit of their own mass, but still way more in absolute terms than something tiny. I wouldn't implement the actual power-law math for an MVP - your own Sept 27 doc already flags "size category (micro/nano/small/large/macro/giga)" as an open item, so I'd just hand-tune a fixed energy-cost and biomass-value per size tier instead of computing an exponent. Keeps it simple and still roughly true to the real pattern (big = expensive in total, efficient per-kg, slow-breeding and hard to wipe out; small = cheap, fast-breeding, volatile).

So the per-turn number crunch becomes: well budget -> producer biomass -> (~10%) available to consumers -> population count derived from biomass / per-individual energy cost for that size tier, capped by the well's slot capacity. All hidden from the player behind the forecast-style text you already planned. Want to lock this as the MVP's model, or is there a part of it you want to simplify further before we treat it as settled?

Biotope vs. habitat, and whether biotope is the right umbrella word: yes - and there's a real, precise reason biotope is the correct pick, not just a nicer-sounding synonym. In actual ecology:

- Habitat is defined relative to a species - "the habitat of lineage X" means wherever X happens to live. Two different lineages in the same physical spot can be described as having different habitats, because the word is inherently species-centric.
- Biotope is the place's own objective physical/chemical conditions - temperature, medium, available energy - independent of who's living there or whether anyone is. It's the thing many different lineages can each judge their own fit against.
- There's a classic textbook pairing here too: biotope + biocoenosis (the community of organisms occupying it) = ecosystem. You probably don't need "biocoenosis" as a game term, but it's worth knowing "ecosystem" is the natural word later if you ever want to talk about "this biotope plus everyone currently living in it" as one thing.
- Adjacent terms if you want alternatives: ecotope (near-synonym for biotope, landscape-ecology flavor, more obscure), niche (not a place at all - a species' functional role, what it eats and how, which you're already heading toward with the player-action brainstorm), biome (too large-scale - a whole climate region like "tundra," not a single local spot).

So: biotope is correct as the umbrella term for the shared, species-independent stat bundle, and "habitat" staying reserved for the ship-deck sense you already have (band + well, from the species' perspective of what it needs) is actually the right split, not a collision.

Multi-step food chains and peer competition - here's a clean mechanism:

Use the size tiers you just picked as the whole ordering mechanism: a lineage can only feed on living biomass from a lineage in a smaller size tier than itself (non-living wells like sunlight or chemical seeps are always available regardless of size). That alone gives you plankton -> sardine -> tuna -> shark for free, with zero hand-authored predator/prey pairs - it falls directly out of the size axis. At each step, apply the same 10%-of-standing-biomass rule you already liked, now sourced from "all smaller, co-located lineages" rather than a single non-living well.

For two peer hunters (A and B, same tier, both eating the tier below, not each other): this is a real named thing in ecology - exploitative competition (as opposed to interference competition, where they'd fight directly). The simplest turn-based version: split the available prey biomass proportionally to each lineage's own current biomass - A's share = prey_pool * (A_biomass / (A_biomass + B_biomass)). Bigger population claims proportionally more of the shared food, which is both realistic and gives you the "two lineages slowly squeeze each other out" dynamic for free.

That also quietly answers something I hadn't connected yet: it gives the mutation-driven niche-escape idea from earlier an actual reason to fire, not just a random die roll - a lineage losing a food competition is exactly the population under the most pressure to find a fringe-adjacent niche nobody's contesting yet. Competitive pressure becomes the thing that makes colonization attempts more likely, not just population size and base mutation rate.

The math - does a small imbalance snowball? Here's the actual arithmetic, and the answer is more interesting than "yes it snowballs":

Setup: lineage A = 105 biomass, B = 100 biomass (a 5% lead for A), a shared pool of 20 biomass/turn, split purely proportional to current biomass, added directly.

- Turn 1: A gets 20x(105/205)=10.244, B gets 20x(100/205)=9.756 -> A=115.244, B=109.756. Ratio A:B = 1.0500
- Turn 2: A gets 20x(115.244/225)=10.244, B gets 9.756 -> A=125.488, B=119.512. Ratio = 1.0500
- Turn 3: A=135.732, B=129.268. Ratio = 1.0500
- Turn 4: A=145.976, B=139.024. Ratio = 1.0500
- Turn 5: A=156.220, B=148.780. Ratio = 1.0500

The ratio never moves. That's not a rounding coincidence - it's provable algebraically: if you add to each side an amount exactly proportional to its current share of a fixed pool, the new ratio equals the old ratio, always. So a pure, continuous, proportional split is actually a neutral equilibrium - a small lead is permanent but never compounds into exclusion on its own. That's the opposite of what real competitive-exclusion dynamics (Gause's law) usually predict, and the reason those real models do diverge is that they add something this formula doesn't: a genuine efficiency/fitness difference between the competitors.

Two things would break that stillness in your actual game, though, both legitimate:

1. Discreteness and randomness. You're tracking whole individuals, not continuous biomass - so every turn's outcome has to round, and real reproduction/survival has some randomness baked in. A random nudge most turns, sometimes favoring A, sometimes B, with no restoring force, is exactly the Gambler's Ruin problem - and over many turns, one side eventually drifts to zero purely by chance, even with no inherent advantage. This is a real, named phenomenon in ecology (Hubbell's neutral theory of biodiversity treats whole community turnover this way) and in genetics (genetic drift). It's slow, not an instant snowball from a 5% gap - but it is a real long-run outcome of the model as specified.
2. Any genuine asymmetry - a trait-driven efficiency difference, a different maintenance cost, a worse environmental fit, or a deliberate player action like culling - breaks the tie once, and from that point on the new ratio is what gets locked in (not runaway acceleration, just a permanent new baseline).

Practically: the model won't feel unstable or snowbally on its own, which is good for not having populations flicker in and out chaotically - but it also means genuine shifts (a trait advantage, a hazard, a player action) are what actually move the needle, and they'll move it permanently rather than temporarily. That seems like a desirable property for "is this fun to interact with," since it makes player actions feel consequential rather than getting washed out by the simulation correcting itself.

Your scaled numbers (biomass x1000/tier, metabolism x100/tier) are a reasonable, realistic-flavored abstraction of the real fly/elephant data and I wouldn't change them. The actual problem is narrower: it's the competition-split formula, not the size scale. Right now competition splits a shared well proportional to raw standing biomass - and because biomass spans 10^12 across your five tiers while metabolism only spans 10^8, a single Massive individual's aggregate biomass can be so disproportionately large that it mathematically swallows almost the entire shared pool against any same-well competitor, regardless of how many of them there are. 1. Split competition by metabolic need, not standing biomass. What should determine "who gets fed" is closer to "who's hungry," not "who's already big" - and metabolic need is the gentler of your two scales (100x/tier vs. 1000x/tier), so it's far less prone to total domination by one tier.

Forking - trigger vs. continuous probability: a discrete "trigger condition" is the wrong model here, and it would also be inconsistent with everything else built so far, which has all been continuous per-turn probability (mutation rate, competition splits) rather than threshold events. Fold forking into the exact same mutation-rate machinery, applied to a specific question: "does this lineage colonize an adjacent biome this turn?"

For each lineage, in each biome it currently occupies, check every other biome that's exactly one trait-flip away and not yet occupied by this lineage. For each candidate, compute the chance of a successful colonizing mutation appearing somewhere in the population this turn as:

P(fork this turn) = 1 - (1 - mutation_rate)^N

where N is the lineage's population count in that biome. This is the actual textbook population-genetics formula for "does a given rare mutation appear at least once in a population of size N" - so it's not an invented game mechanic, it's borrowed directly from the real thing. It does exactly what was wanted without any extra rule: at low N it stays small even with a boosted mutation rate, and it climbs toward near-certainty as N grows, with no hand-tuned threshold required anywhere.

Simulating the actual split: convert a small, fixed number of individuals - recommended literally 1 for the MVP - out of the origin population into a brand-new, separately-tracked lineage established in the destination biome, already carrying the one differing trait that made the jump possible.
